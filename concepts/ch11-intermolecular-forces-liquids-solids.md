# Chapter 11 Concept Map: Intermolecular Forces, Liquids, and Solids

## Source and Scope

**Reference:** instructor's own lecture notes (Yu Wang, University of Louisiana at Lafayette),
Chapter 11 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry. **Note:** covers
Intermolecular Forces (polar and non-polar interactions, hydrogen bonds), Properties of Liquids
(surface tension, viscosity, water), Solids (crystal types and structures), and Phase Changes and
Diagrams. Simple Markdown only — no images, diagrams, or orz containers.

## Chapter Summary

Chapter 11 explains bulk-phase behavior (why some substances are gases, liquids, or solids at room
temperature, and why they have the melting points, boiling points, and mechanical properties they
do) in terms of the intermolecular forces acting between particles. It classifies these forces —
dipole-dipole forces (between polar molecules, built on Chapter 10's polarity result), dispersion
forces (from temporary induced dipoles, present in all molecules), and hydrogen bonding (a
especially strong dipole-dipole case involving H bonded to N, O, or F) — and ranks their relative
strength.

These forces then explain liquid properties (surface tension, viscosity, water's anomalous
behavior) and solid-state classification (molecular, covalent-network, ionic, and metallic solids,
plus crystal unit cells and coordination numbers). The chapter closes with phase changes and phase
diagrams, introducing molar heats of vaporization/fusion/sublimation (reusing Chapter 6's
q = nΔH logic) and the Clausius-Clapeyron equation for vapor pressure vs. temperature. This
chapter's intermolecular-force framework is the direct explanatory basis for Chapter 12's solution
chemistry ("like dissolves like" is an intermolecular-force statement).

## Section-Level Concept Map and Objectives

### 1. Intermolecular Forces

**Learning objectives:**
- Distinguish intermolecular forces from intramolecular (bonding) forces.
- Identify dipole-dipole forces, dispersion forces, and hydrogen bonds given a molecule's
  structure.
- Rank the relative strength of ion-dipole, hydrogen bonding, dipole-dipole, and dispersion
  forces.

**Concept flow:**
- Intermolecular forces (between molecules) vs. intramolecular forces (within a molecule, e.g.,
  covalent bonds)
- Polar molecules (from Ch. 10) → dipole-dipole forces; all molecules → dispersion forces
  (temporary induced dipoles, increasing with polarizability)
- Hydrogen bonding (H bonded to N, O, or F, attracted to another electronegative atom) → a
  special, especially strong dipole-dipole case
- Overall strength ranking: ion-dipole > hydrogen bond > dipole-dipole > dispersion force

### 2. Properties of Liquids

**Learning objectives:**
- Define surface tension and viscosity, and relate both to intermolecular force strength.
- Distinguish cohesion from adhesion, and explain capillary action.
- Explain water's unusually high melting/boiling point, specific heat, and maximum-density
  behavior in terms of hydrogen bonding.

**Concept flow:**
- Surface tension (resistance to increasing surface area) and viscosity (resistance to flow) →
  both increase with stronger intermolecular forces
- Cohesion (like-molecule attraction) vs. adhesion (unlike-molecule attraction) → explains
  capillary action
- Water's four hydrogen bonds per molecule → anomalously high melting/boiling point and specific
  heat; maximum density at 4 °C

### 3. Solids

**Learning objectives:**
- Distinguish crystalline solids from amorphous solids.
- Classify a solid as molecular, covalent-network, ionic, or metallic, and describe each type's
  typical hardness, melting point, and conductivity.
- Identify simple cubic, body-centered cubic, and face-centered cubic unit cells, and calculate
  coordination number and atoms per unit cell.

**Concept flow:**
- Crystalline (long-range order) vs. amorphous (no long-range order) solids
- Solid type classification by the force holding it together: molecular (IMF, soft/low-melting),
  covalent network (covalent bonds, hard/high-melting), ionic (ionic bonds, hard/brittle),
  metallic (metallic bonds, variable hardness, good conductor)
- Unit cells (SCC, BCC, FCC) → coordination number and atom count per cell determined by how atoms
  touch along edges/diagonals; close-packing (hexagonal or cubic) as the most efficient
  arrangements

### 4. Phase Changes and Diagrams

**Learning objectives:**
- Define evaporation/condensation, dynamic equilibrium, and equilibrium vapor pressure.
- Calculate heat absorbed/released in a phase change using molar heat of vaporization, fusion, or
  sublimation.
- Apply the Clausius-Clapeyron equation to relate vapor pressure and temperature.
- Interpret a phase diagram, including the triple point and critical point.

**Concept flow:**
- Evaporation/condensation → dynamic equilibrium at a given temperature → equilibrium vapor
  pressure
- Molar heat of vaporization/fusion/sublimation → q = n × ΔH for a phase change (same logic as
  Ch. 6 reaction enthalpy)
  - Hess's law: ΔH_sub = ΔH_fus + ΔH_vap
- Clausius-Clapeyron equation (ln P vs. 1/T) → relates vapor pressure to temperature using
  ΔH_vap
- Phase diagram → regions (solid/liquid/gas), boundary lines (two-phase coexistence), triple point
  (all three phases coexist), critical point (beyond which gas cannot be liquefied by pressure
  alone)

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Assuming all polar molecules have hydrogen bonding | Require students to check specifically for H bonded directly to N, O, or F before classifying an interaction as hydrogen bonding, rather than inferring it from polarity alone. |
| Ranking dispersion forces as always weaker than dipole-dipole forces regardless of molecule size | Note the exception explicitly: dispersion forces can exceed dipole-dipole forces in large, highly polarizable nonpolar molecules — polarizability, not just polarity, matters. |
| Confusing coordination number with the number of atoms per unit cell | Keep the two questions separate: coordination number counts nearest neighbors to one atom; atoms per unit cell counts total atoms belonging to that cell (accounting for shared corners/faces). |
| Misreading a phase diagram, especially confusing the triple point with the critical point | Anchor to their distinct definitions: the triple point is where three phases coexist; the critical point is the temperature/pressure limit beyond which gas and liquid become indistinguishable. |
| Using ΔH_vap in the Clausius-Clapeyron equation with inconsistent units (J vs. kJ) against R | Require an explicit units check on ΔH_vap and R before substituting into the Clausius-Clapeyron equation. |
