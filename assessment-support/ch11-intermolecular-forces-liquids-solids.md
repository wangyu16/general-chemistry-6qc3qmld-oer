# Chapter 11 Assessment Guide: Intermolecular Forces, Liquids, and Solids

## Source and Format

**Reference:** Yu Wang's General Chemistry Lecture Notes, Chapter 11 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry, second semester. **Note:** this is design guidance for building assessments — question *guides* an instructor or generator can instantiate into many concrete questions — not a finished question bank or answer key.

## General Assessment Priorities

- Prioritize **structure-to-force reasoning** (given a molecule, correctly identify every applicable force) over rote memorization of a strength ranking — the ranking is only useful once the forces present are correctly identified.
- Test the **unit-cell atom-counting method** as a procedure (corner = 1/8, face = 1/2, body = 1), not as a memorized final number, since the same method generalizes beyond the three named cells.
- Weight Clausius–Clapeyron and heat-of-phase-change problems toward **multi-step, unit-tracking** calculations, mirroring the stoichiometry/thermochemistry skills built in earlier chapters.
- Include at least one question per objective that asks students to **explain why**, not just calculate — this chapter is unusually conceptual (structure → force → bulk property) and purely numeric assessment would miss that.

## Objective 11.1a: Identify intermolecular forces from molecular structure

### Target understanding

A student who has mastered this can look at a molecular formula or Lewis structure and correctly list every intermolecular force present (==dispersion always; dipole-dipole if polar; hydrogen bonding only if H is bonded directly to N, O, or F==), without conflating intramolecular (covalent) bonds with intermolecular forces.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — identify forces present**
- Variables & ranges: a molecule drawn from a pool spanning nonpolar (CH₄, CO₂, Br₂), polar without H-bonding (HCl, acetone), and H-bonding-capable (H₂O, NH₃, CH₃OH, HF) categories.
- Constraint: the correct answer set must include dispersion in every case, plus dipole-dipole if polar, plus hydrogen bonding only if H–N/O/F is present.
- Contexts: a household chemical, an atmospheric gas, a biomolecule fragment.
- Formats: short-answer (list the forces) or multiple-choice (select all that apply).
- Worked instantiation: "List every intermolecular force present in acetone, $\ce{(CH3)2CO}$." → Dispersion and dipole-dipole (the C=O bond is polar, but there is no H bonded to O, N, or F — the H atoms are on carbon) — no hydrogen bonding.

**2. Inverse — name a molecule with a given force profile**
- Variables & ranges: a target force profile (e.g., "dispersion only," "dipole-dipole and dispersion but no hydrogen bonding," "all three").
- Constraint: the named molecule's structure must actually match the requested profile exactly (no extra or missing force).
- Contexts: everyday solvents, common gases.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Give an example of a molecule with dipole-dipole forces but no hydrogen bonding." → e.g., $\ce{CH3Cl}$ or $\ce{HCl}$ (polar, but no H bonded to N/O/F).

**3. Conceptual / predict-the-trend**
- Variables & ranges: a pair of isomers or near-analogs where only one has an N–H/O–H/F–H bond (e.g., dimethyl ether $\ce{CH3OCH3}$ vs. ethanol $\ce{C2H5OH}$).
- Constraint: student must justify the difference in terms of *where* the H atoms are bonded, not just molecular formula.
- Contexts: comparing common isomer pairs.
- Formats: short-answer or multiple-choice with justification.
- Worked instantiation: "Ethanol and dimethyl ether share the formula C₂H₆O. Which has hydrogen bonding, and why?" → Ethanol (O–H bond present); dimethyl ether's H atoms are all bonded to carbon.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a flawed student claim (e.g., "CH₄ has hydrogen bonds because it contains hydrogen").
- Constraint: the correction must identify the specific rule violated (H must bond to N, O, or F).
- Contexts: common student misconceptions collected from this exact bottleneck.
- Formats: short-answer ("find and fix the error").
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's flagged bottleneck — a false claim that any H-containing molecule hydrogen-bonds.

**5. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: an everyday scenario tied to a specific force (e.g., why nail polish remover — acetone — evaporates faster than water).
- Constraint: answer must connect the observable behavior (evaporation rate) to relative IMF strength.
- Contexts: household products, biological membranes, industrial solvents.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Acetone evaporates faster than water at the same temperature. What does this suggest about their relative intermolecular forces?" → Acetone's forces (dipole-dipole + dispersion, no H-bonding) are weaker than water's (extensive hydrogen bonding), so acetone molecules escape the liquid more easily.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | Give 4–6 molecules spanning all three categories; ask for a complete force list per molecule. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Have groups sort a mixed set of molecule cards into "hydrogen-bonding," "dipole-dipole only," "dispersion only" bins and defend borderline cases. |
| Quiz | Apply/Understand | 3–4 short-answer items, one from each question-guide type above. |
| Exam | Analyze/Evaluate | Pair force identification with a boiling-point-ranking task (links to Objective 11.1b). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Have students research and present the intermolecular forces behind a real product (e.g., why gecko feet stick, using van der Waals forces). |

## Objective 11.1b: Rank intermolecular forces by relative strength

### Target understanding

A student can order a set of molecules or force types by relative strength, correctly applying the general ranking (ion-dipole > hydrogen bond > dipole-dipole > dispersion) while recognizing the exception case where a large, highly polarizable nonpolar molecule can out-rank a small polar one.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — rank by boiling point**
- Variables & ranges: a set of 3–4 substances with a clear force-strength ordering (e.g., Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe — dispersion only, increasing polarizability).
- Constraint: ranking must follow directly from the identified forces, not from memorized boiling points.
- Contexts: noble gases, halogens, small hydrocarbons.
- Formats: short-answer (rank in order) or workout (justify each comparison).
- Worked instantiation: "Rank Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe by increasing boiling point." → Ne < Ar < Kr < Xe (dispersion forces increase with more electrons/polarizability).

**2. Comparison / ranking — mixed force types**
- Variables & ranges: a small set combining a hydrogen-bonding molecule, a dipole-dipole-only molecule, and a large nonpolar molecule.
- Constraint: at least one pair must require the "large nonpolar can beat small polar" exception.
- Contexts: comparing water, HCl, and a large nonpolar hydrocarbon (e.g., decane).
- Formats: workout with justification.
- Worked instantiation: compare $\ce{HCl}$ (dipole-dipole, small) to $\ce{C10H22}$ (nonpolar, large, dispersion-only) — decane's boiling point (174 °C) actually exceeds HCl's (−85 °C), illustrating that size/polarizability can dominate over polarity alone.

**3. Graphical**
- Variables & ranges: a plotted or tabulated boiling-point trend across a period or group (e.g., the hydride boiling-point chart from the study guide).
- Constraint: student must identify which points are anomalous and explain why.
- Contexts: the Group 14–17 hydride boiling-point comparison.
- Formats: short-answer referencing a chart.
- Worked instantiation: "Using the hydride boiling-point chart, explain why H₂O, NH₃, and HF fall above the trend line set by their heavier group members." → Hydrogen bonding, absent in the heavier hydrides, adds substantial extra attraction only for these three.

**4. Estimation / limiting case**
- Variables & ranges: an extreme comparison (e.g., a monatomic noble gas vs. a large polymer chain).
- Constraint: answer should reason qualitatively about the limiting behavior, not require exact data.
- Contexts: comparing helium to a long-chain hydrocarbon or polymer.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Would you expect liquid helium or a melted long-chain wax to have stronger intermolecular attraction? Why?" → The wax — dispersion forces scale with molecular size/electron count, and a long chain vastly exceeds helium's 2 electrons.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | Rank 4–5 given substances by boiling point using identified forces. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate a "trick" pair where size beats polarity; require textual justification. |
| Quiz | Understand/Apply | One straightforward ranking item, one exception-case item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 11.1a: identify forces, then rank. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Have students find and explain one real "surprising" boiling-point comparison from a reference table. |

## Objective 11.2a: Relate surface tension, viscosity, cohesion, and adhesion to intermolecular force strength

### Target understanding

A student can predict how surface tension or viscosity should compare between two liquids based on their relative intermolecular force strength, and can explain capillary action in terms of the cohesion/adhesion balance.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — predict relative surface tension/viscosity**
- Variables & ranges: pairs of liquids differing in IMF strength (e.g., water vs. hexane; honey vs. water).
- Constraint: prediction must be justified by the underlying force, not just asserted.
- Contexts: kitchen liquids, motor oils, everyday comparisons.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Which has higher viscosity: honey or water? Why?" → Honey — its sugar molecules form extensive hydrogen-bonding networks, far exceeding water's.

**2. Conceptual — cohesion vs. adhesion**
- Variables & ranges: a capillary-action scenario (paper towel, thin glass tube, plant xylem) with a specified liquid/surface pair.
- Constraint: explanation must correctly identify which attraction (cohesion or adhesion) must dominate.
- Contexts: everyday absorption, biological water transport.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does water rise up a thin glass tube, but mercury is depressed below the surrounding level in the same tube?" → Water's adhesion to glass exceeds its cohesion (rises); mercury's cohesion (strong metallic-like attraction) exceeds its adhesion to glass (depressed).

**3. Temperature effect**
- Variables & ranges: a liquid's viscosity at two different temperatures.
- Constraint: direction of change (viscosity decreases with increasing temperature) must be explained via increased molecular motion overcoming IMF.
- Contexts: motor oil viscosity in cold vs. warm engines.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does motor oil flow more easily when an engine is warm than when it is cold?" → Higher temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy to overcome intermolecular attractions, lowering viscosity.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a flawed claim confusing cohesion and adhesion, or confusing surface tension with viscosity.
- Constraint: correction must restate the precise definitions.
- Contexts: common mix-ups between the two paired concepts.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student claims surface tension and viscosity are the same property. Explain the distinction." → Surface tension resists increasing surface *area* (a 2-D, surface-only effect); viscosity resists *flow* (a bulk, 3-D effect).

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 predict-and-justify items across surface tension, viscosity, and capillary action. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Have groups design (on paper) an experiment to compare two liquids' viscosities. |
| Quiz | Understand | One definition-matching item, one prediction item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require justification tied to specific IMF type, not just "stronger forces." |
| Project/activity | Create | Have students measure (qualitatively) and explain a household capillary-action demonstration. |

## Objective 11.2b: Explain water's anomalous properties via hydrogen bonding

### Target understanding

A student can list water's unusual properties (high boiling point for its size, high specific heat, maximum density at 4 °C, expansion on freezing) and ==trace each one back to its extensive hydrogen-bonding network==.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — explain one anomaly**
- Variables & ranges: one of water's four anomalous properties named in the study guide.
- Constraint: explanation must reference hydrogen bonding specifically, not "special forces" vaguely.
- Contexts: climate moderation, ice floating, aquatic ecosystems.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does ice float on liquid water?" → Hydrogen bonds lock water molecules into an open, spacious lattice in ice, making solid water *less* dense than the liquid.

**2. Comparison / ranking**
- Variables & ranges: compare water's boiling point to a same-size or larger nonpolar/weakly-polar molecule (e.g., CH₄, or Ne).
- Constraint: must invoke the size/molar-mass mismatch as the surprising element.
- Contexts: molar-mass comparisons.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Water (18 g/mol) boils at 100 °C; methane (16 g/mol, similar mass) boils at −161.5 °C. Explain the huge difference." → Water hydrogen-bonds extensively; methane, nonpolar, has only weak dispersion forces.

**3. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: a climate or biological consequence of one specific anomaly.
- Constraint: answer must connect the macroscopic effect to the correct microscopic cause.
- Contexts: coastal climate moderation, winter lake ecology.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why do coastal cities have milder temperature swings than inland cities at the same latitude?" → Water's high specific heat (from hydrogen bonding) lets large bodies of water absorb/release heat slowly, moderating nearby air temperature.

**4. Multi-step / synthesis**
- Variables & ranges: connect water's hydrogen bonding to its phase diagram's negative-sloped solid-liquid boundary (links to Objective 11.4c).
- Constraint: must connect density anomaly to phase-diagram slope direction.
- Contexts: ice skating, glacial ice under pressure.
- Formats: workout/short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Explain why increasing pressure slightly lowers ice's melting point, using water's hydrogen-bonding structure." → Ice's hydrogen-bonded lattice is less dense than liquid water; added pressure favors the denser liquid phase, shifting the equilibrium toward melting at a lower temperature than at 1 atm.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand/Apply | List-and-explain all four anomalies. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate which anomaly has the largest real-world consequence. |
| Quiz | Understand | One anomaly, explained in 2–3 sentences. |
| Exam | Synthesize | Connect to Objective 11.4c (phase diagram slope). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research a specific ecosystem/climate effect of one water anomaly. |

## Objective 11.3a: Classify solids and predict their properties

### Target understanding

Given a substance's composition and bonding, a student can classify it as molecular, covalent-network, ionic, or metallic, and correctly predict its relative hardness, melting point, and electrical conductivity (including the graphite exception).

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify and predict**
- Variables & ranges: a substance drawn from all four solid categories (e.g., dry ice, diamond, KBr, aluminum).
- Constraint: classification must be justified by bond type, and property predictions must follow from the table in the study guide.
- Contexts: common lab and household substances.
- Formats: short-answer or multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "Classify KBr as molecular, covalent-network, ionic, or metallic, and predict its melting point range." → Ionic (metal + nonmetal, electron transfer); high melting point, hard but brittle, conducts only when molten or dissolved.

**2. Comparison / ranking**
- Variables & ranges: rank 3–4 solids by expected melting point.
- Constraint: ranking must follow from bond-type classification, not memorized values.
- Contexts: comparing a molecular solid, an ionic solid, and a covalent-network solid.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Rank dry ice (CO₂), table salt (NaCl), and diamond \(C) by increasing melting point." → dry ice (molecular, weakest) < NaCl (ionic) < diamond (covalent-network, strongest).

**3. Error analysis / exception case**
- Variables & ranges: graphite's conductivity, an apparent exception to "covalent-network solids don't conduct."
- Constraint: explanation must reference graphite's delocalized electrons between layers.
- Contexts: pencil lead, electrodes.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Diamond and graphite are both covalent-network forms of carbon, yet graphite conducts electricity and diamond does not. Why?" → Graphite has delocalized π electrons that move freely within its layered sheets; diamond's electrons are all localized in fixed sigma bonds.

**4. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: a material-selection scenario (e.g., choosing a solid for high heat resistance, or for electrical wiring).
- Constraint: choice must be justified by the relevant solid-type property.
- Contexts: engineering material choice.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is copper, not diamond, used for electrical wiring, even though diamond is harder?" → Copper is metallic and conducts well via delocalized electrons; diamond is a covalent-network insulator despite its hardness.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | Classify 5–6 substances and predict one property each. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate borderline cases (e.g., graphite) and defend the classification. |
| Quiz | Understand/Apply | Table-style matching: substance → type → property. |
| Exam | Analyze/Evaluate | Require justification, not just the classification label. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research a real material and justify its solid-type classification and use. |

## Objective 11.3b: Count atoms per unit cell and determine coordination number

### Target understanding

A student can apply the ==corner (1/8), face (1/2), and body (1) sharing fractions== to correctly compute the number of atoms per unit cell for SCC, BCC, and FCC, and can state each cell's coordination number.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — count atoms in a named cell**
- Variables & ranges: SCC, BCC, or FCC, specified.
- Constraint: student must show the sharing-fraction arithmetic, not just recall the final number.
- Contexts: any of the three cubic cells.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (BCC = 2 atoms/cell).

**2. Inverse — identify the cell from a given atom count or coordination number**
- Variables & ranges: given "4 atoms per cell" or "coordination number 6," identify the cell type.
- Constraint: must reason from the definition, not guess-and-check.
- Contexts: unknown-crystal identification.
- Formats: multiple-choice or short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A cubic unit cell has 4 atoms per cell. Which cell type is it?" → FCC.

**3. Multi-step / synthesis**
- Variables & ranges: given a cell type and a real atomic radius, calculate a derived quantity (e.g., edge length via the touching-atom geometry) — appropriate for a stronger cohort.
- Constraint: only assign if the geometric touching relationship (edge, face diagonal, or body diagonal) has been explicitly taught/reviewed.
- Contexts: metal crystal structures (e.g., polonium as SCC, iron as BCC, copper as FCC).
- Formats: workout (optional/advanced).
- Worked instantiation: identify which cubic cell iron adopts at room temperature (BCC) and state its coordination number (8).

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a flawed count (e.g., counting all 8 corner atoms as whole atoms).
- Constraint: correction must restate the correct sharing fraction and why it applies.
- Contexts: the exact bottleneck flagged in the concept map (forgetting corner/face sharing).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says SCC has 8 atoms per cell because there are 8 corners. What is the error?" → Each corner atom is shared among 8 adjacent cells, contributing only 1/8 to this cell; the correct total is 1 atom per cell.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | Compute atoms/cell and coordination number for all three cubic cells. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Have groups physically model (with balls or drawings) the sharing fractions. |
| Quiz | Apply | One computation item per cell type, or one inverse-identification item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Pair with a real-metal identification question. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research which cubic cell a specific real metal adopts and verify with the coordination number. |

## Objective 11.4a: Apply heat-of-phase-change calculations and define equilibrium vapor pressure

### Target understanding

A student can calculate the heat absorbed or released in a phase change using molar heat of vaporization, fusion, or sublimation, and can explain evaporation, condensation, and dynamic equilibrium in words.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — heat of vaporization/fusion**
- Variables & ranges: mass (1–500 g) of a substance with a given $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ or $\Delta H_\text{fus}$ (0.5–45 kJ/mol range).
- Constraint: student must convert mass to moles before applying the molar heat value.
- Contexts: boiling water, melting ice, vaporizing a solvent.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (10.0 g water, $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ = 40.66 kJ/mol → 22.6 kJ absorbed).

**2. Inverse — solve for mass or $\Delta H$**
- Variables & ranges: given heat absorbed and $\Delta H_\text{vap}$, solve for mass; or given heat and mass, solve for $\Delta H_\text{vap}$.
- Constraint: same unit-conversion chain as the forward version, solved in reverse.
- Contexts: calorimetry-style setups.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "If 45.2 kJ vaporizes an unknown mass of water ($\Delta H_\text{vap}$ = 40.66 kJ/mol), what mass was vaporized?" → 20.0 g.

**3. Conceptual — dynamic equilibrium**
- Variables & ranges: a sealed vs. open container scenario.
- Constraint: explanation must state that both evaporation and condensation continue at equilibrium (rates equal, not zero).
- Contexts: a sealed jar vs. an open glass of water.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does water in a sealed jar not fully evaporate, while water in an open glass eventually does?" → In the sealed jar, evaporation and condensation reach dynamic equilibrium; in the open glass, escaping vapor is never replaced, so evaporation continues net-forward.

**4. Multi-step / synthesis**
- Variables & ranges: combine a sublimation calculation with Hess's law ($\Delta H_\text{sub} = \Delta H_\text{fus} + \Delta H_\text{vap}$).
- Constraint: must correctly add the two given values before converting to a mass-based heat quantity.
- Contexts: dry ice or another common subliming solid.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given $\Delta H_\text{fus}$ and $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ for a substance, compute $\Delta H_\text{sub}$ and then the heat needed to sublime a given mass.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 mass↔heat conversions across vaporization, fusion, and sublimation. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss the sealed-vs-open-container equilibrium scenario. |
| Quiz | Apply | One forward, one inverse calculation. |
| Exam | Synthesize | Combine with Hess's law for sublimation. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Explain a real refrigeration or evaporative-cooling application. |

## Objective 11.4b: Apply the Clausius–Clapeyron equation

### Target understanding

A student can use the Clausius–Clapeyron equation to solve for an unknown vapor pressure or temperature, given $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ and one known (P, T) pair, ==tracking units (especially T in kelvin) correctly throughout==.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — solve for P₂ at a new temperature**
- Variables & ranges: $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ (15–45 kJ/mol), one known (P₁, T₁) pair, a target T₂.
- Constraint: T must be converted to kelvin; final answer's units must match P₁'s units.
- Contexts: common solvents (diethyl ether, ethanol, benzene).
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (diethyl ether, 400 mmHg at 27 °C → 1,770 mmHg at 77 °C).

**2. Inverse — solve for T₂ at a target pressure**
- Variables & ranges: given $\Delta H_\text{vap}$, one known (P₁, T₁) pair, and a target P₂ (e.g., 760 mmHg, to find the normal boiling point).
- Constraint: student must isolate T₂ algebraically from the log relationship.
- Contexts: finding a substance's normal boiling point from one other data point.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Using diethyl ether's data, estimate the temperature at which its vapor pressure reaches 760 mmHg (1 atm) — its normal boiling point." → Solve the Clausius–Clapeyron equation for T₂ with P₂ = 760 mmHg (answer near 35 °C, consistent with the study guide's figure).

**3. Inverse — solve for $\Delta H_\text{vap}$**
- Variables & ranges: given two (P, T) pairs for the same substance, solve for $\Delta H_\text{vap}$.
- Constraint: correct algebraic rearrangement to isolate $\Delta H_\text{vap}$.
- Contexts: experimental vapor-pressure data at two temperatures.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given vapor pressures at two known temperatures for an unknown liquid, back-calculate its $\Delta H_\text{vap}$.

**4. Graphical**
- Variables & ranges: the vapor-pressure-vs-temperature figure from the study guide (or a similar curve for another substance).
- Constraint: student must correctly read off an approximate value or identify the normal boiling point (where the curve crosses 760 mmHg).
- Contexts: reading a plotted vapor-pressure curve.
- Formats: short-answer referencing the figure.
- Worked instantiation: "From the vapor pressure curve, approximately what temperature gives diethyl ether a vapor pressure of 760 mmHg?" → About 35 °C.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 forward/inverse Clausius–Clapeyron problems with different unknowns. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Discuss why T must be in kelvin (connects to Ch. 5 gas-law habits). |
| Quiz | Apply | One straightforward P₂-solving item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require solving for the "wrong" variable (T₂ or $\Delta H_\text{vap}$), not just P₂. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Look up real vapor-pressure data for a substance and verify $\Delta H_\text{vap}$ from two points. |

## Objective 11.4c: Interpret a phase diagram

### Target understanding

A student can read a phase diagram to identify the phase present at a given (T, P), locate the triple point and critical point, and explain water's unusual negative-sloped solid-liquid boundary.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — identify the phase at a point**
- Variables & ranges: a (T, P) coordinate placed in the solid, liquid, or vapor region of the water phase diagram.
- Constraint: student must reference the actual diagram, not guess.
- Contexts: the water phase diagram from the study guide.
- Formats: short-answer or multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "At 0.001 atm and 50 °C, what phase is water in?" → Vapor (below the sublimation/vaporization curve at that low pressure).

**2. Conceptual — identify the triple point / critical point**
- Variables & ranges: ask for the definition and approximate location of either point.
- Constraint: answer must state both coordinate values and the defining condition (three phases coexisting, or liquid/vapor indistinguishability).
- Contexts: the water phase diagram.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "What is unique about water's critical point (374 °C, 218 atm)?" → Above this temperature and pressure, liquid and vapor phases become indistinguishable — no amount of pressure can liquefy water vapor above 374 °C.

**3. Predict-the-trend — following a path on the diagram**
- Variables & ranges: a starting (T, P) and a direction of change (increase T at constant P, or increase P at constant T).
- Constraint: student must trace the correct path across phase boundaries in the correct order.
- Contexts: heating ice at low pressure (sublimation) vs. at 1 atm (melting then boiling).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Starting at −10 °C and 0.001 atm, what happens to water as temperature is slowly raised at constant pressure?" → It sublimes directly from solid to vapor, never passing through a liquid phase at this low pressure.

**4. Comparison — water vs. a "normal" substance**
- Variables & ranges: contrast water's negative-sloped solid-liquid boundary with a substance having the more common positive slope.
- Constraint: explanation must connect the slope direction to relative solid/liquid density.
- Contexts: ice skating (water) vs. melting paraffin wax under pressure.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does increasing pressure on ice lower its melting point, while increasing pressure on solid wax raises its melting point?" → Ice is less dense than liquid water (unique among common substances), so pressure favors the denser liquid; wax's solid is denser than its liquid, so pressure favors the solid instead.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | Identify the phase at 4–5 different (T, P) points on the water diagram. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate why water's negative slope is unusual, using the density argument. |
| Quiz | Understand | Define triple point and critical point in one's own words. |
| Exam | Analyze/Evaluate | Trace a full heating/pressurizing path across multiple phase boundaries. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Compare water's phase diagram to CO₂'s (no liquid phase at 1 atm) as an extension. |

## Rubric Themes for Chapter 11

| Evidence of mastery | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Correct force identification | Student lists dispersion in *every* case, adds dipole-dipole only if polar, and adds hydrogen bonding only with H bonded to N, O, or F — never overgeneralizing. |
| Force strength used to justify predictions | Boiling point, viscosity, and surface tension comparisons are justified by naming the specific force(s) involved, not by "one has stronger forces" alone. |
